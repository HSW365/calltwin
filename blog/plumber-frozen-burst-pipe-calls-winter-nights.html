How Plumbers Can Catch Frozen and Burst Pipe Calls on Winter Nights

The first hard freeze of the year sends a wave of calls to plumbers, and a lot of them come in after dark, when a homeowner walks into the basement and hears water running. Here is how to get ready so those calls turn into jobs instead of voicemails.

Why winter nights are different

Most of the year, a plumbing call can wait until morning. A dripping faucet or a slow drain will still be there at 8am. A burst supply line won't wait. Water is coming through a ceiling or across a finished basement, and the homeowner is scared and calling every plumber they can find.

That caller is not shopping on price. They want someone to pick up, tell them what happens next, and show up. Whoever answers first is usually the one who gets the job, and often the repipe, the insulation work and the next ten years of service calls that follow.

The problem is that those calls come in when you're asleep, at dinner with your family, or already under a house on another emergency.

Get ready before the first hard freeze

The week before the forecast drops below freezing is the time to set things up, not the night it happens.

  • Stock the truck. PEX, fittings, crimp rings or push-to-connect couplings, pipe insulation, heat tape and a wet vac. A burst-pipe night is not the night to make a supply house run.
  • Decide your night rules. Will you go out after 9pm? After midnight? Only for active leaks, or for any frozen pipe? Write it down so you don't decide half-asleep.
  • Set your after-hours rate. Know your emergency trip charge before the phone rings so you can tell the customer when you call back.
  • Line up backup. If you have a tech or a buddy in the trade who'll split night calls, agree on it now.
  • Update your voicemail and website. Say plainly that you take emergency calls and how to reach you.

Sort the calls: tonight, first thing, or later

Not every winter call is an emergency, and you'll burn out fast if you treat them all that way. A simple sort helps:

  • Go tonight: water actively leaking that the customer can't stop, a burst line in a finished space, no water to the whole house in a home with small kids or elderly people.
  • First thing in the morning: a frozen pipe with no leak yet, where the customer has shut off the water or the pipe is accessible and they can keep the area warm.
  • Schedule it: insulation, heat tape, relocating lines in exterior walls, outdoor spigot replacement. These are good jobs, but they can be booked for a weekday.

The only way to sort calls is to know what's going on, which means you need the details whether you pick up or not.

The details you need from every caller

Whether you answer yourself, a family member answers, or something else picks up, get the same five things every time:

  • Name and a callback number (ask for a cell, not the home line in a flooded basement)
  • The address, including the town
  • What they see: active leak, no water, or a pipe they think is frozen
  • Where it is: basement, crawlspace, kitchen wall, attic, outside
  • Whether the water is shut off, and whether they know where the main shutoff is

With those five answers you can decide in thirty seconds whether to get dressed or call them back at 7am.

Tell customers where the shutoff is, before they need it

A lot of burst-pipe damage happens in the minutes a homeowner spends looking for the main valve. Anything you do to shorten that helps the customer and makes your job smaller when you arrive.

Put a short note in your recorded greeting and on your website: if water is leaking, shut off the main valve, usually where the water line comes into the house in the basement or crawlspace, or near the water heater. Tag the main valve on every service call this fall and show the homeowner where it is. Customers remember the plumber who did that.

Call back fast, even if you aren't going out

The worst outcome is a caller who never hears back. Even if you've decided not to go out until morning, a two-minute call back keeps the job. Tell them what to do tonight, when you'll be there, and roughly what the trip will cost. Most people will wait for a plumber who called them back over gambling on one who didn't.

If you can't take it at all, say so and refer them. That customer will remember you were straight with them.

Run your own numbers

Look at your call log from last winter's first cold week. Count the calls that came in after hours or that you didn't answer. Then look at your invoices from that week and find your average emergency ticket. Multiply the two. That's roughly what was on the table on the nights the phone rang and nobody picked up. Your numbers will be your own, but it's worth knowing them before this winter starts.
