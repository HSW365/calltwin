No Heat in Your Warren or Hunterdon County Home? Check These Things First

The first cold nights of fall are when a lot of heating systems get switched on for the first time in months, and when a lot of them don't come on. Before you assume the worst, a few quick checks can sometimes bring the heat back or at least tell you what's wrong. Here's what to look at, in order, and when to stop and call.

Safety first: gas smell or carbon monoxide alarm

If you smell gas, don't touch light switches, the thermostat or anything that could make a spark. Get everyone out of the house, then call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Don't go back in until they say it's safe.

If a carbon monoxide alarm goes off, get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911. Don't try to troubleshoot the heating system first. A heating system that may be leaking combustion gases needs to be checked by a professional before it runs again.

1. Check the thermostat

It sounds too simple, but it's one of the most common causes of a no-heat call in the fall.

  • Mode: make sure it's set to Heat, not Cool or Off. Many thermostats get left on Cool all summer.
  • Setting: set it a few degrees above the current room temperature and wait several minutes.
  • Batteries: a blank or dim screen often means dead batteries. Replace them and try again.
  • Schedule: programmable and smart thermostats can be holding a schedule or an away setting. Check that it isn't overriding you.

2. Check the power

Gas, oil and electric systems all need electricity to run.

  • Breaker: look at your electrical panel for a tripped breaker labeled furnace, boiler or heat. Reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and call.
  • Service switch: most furnaces and boilers have an on/off switch that looks like a light switch, usually on or near the unit or at the top of the basement stairs. It's easy to bump off by accident.
  • Furnace door: many furnaces have a safety switch that keeps them off if the front panel isn't seated properly. Make sure the panel is fully closed.

3. Forced-air furnace checks

  • Filter: a badly clogged filter can choke airflow and cause the furnace to shut itself off. If it's gray and caked with dust, replace it.
  • Vents and registers: make sure supply vents and return grilles aren't closed or blocked by furniture or rugs.
  • Condensate drain: high-efficiency furnaces drain water through a small plastic line, sometimes into a pump. If that line is clogged or the pump is full, a safety switch can stop the furnace. Water around the base of the furnace is a sign of this. Call rather than taking it apart.

4. Boiler and radiator checks

  • Pressure gauge: most hot water boilers have a gauge on the front. Note the reading and tell us when you call. Don't try to add water or bleed the system if you're not sure how.
  • Some rooms cold, others warm: this can point to air in the lines, a zone valve or a circulator problem rather than the boiler itself. Tell us which rooms or floors are affected.
  • Leaks or puddles: water around the boiler or under a radiator needs attention. Shut off the boiler and call.

5. Oil heat checks

If your home heats with oil, look at the gauge on the tank. An empty or near-empty tank is a common cause of no heat. If the tank has oil and the burner has shut down, there's usually a red reset button on the burner. Press it once. If the burner doesn't start, or starts and shuts off again, don't keep pressing it. Repeated resets can push extra oil into the burner and create a bigger problem. Leave it off and call.

6. Pilot lights and error codes

Older gas equipment may have a standing pilot. If it's out, only relight it by following the instructions printed on the unit, and never if you smell gas. Newer furnaces and boilers often have a small light that blinks a code; the chart for that code is usually on the inside of the panel door. Write down the blink pattern or code. It helps the technician come prepared.

Heat pumps and geothermal systems

Heat pumps and geothermal systems often have auxiliary or emergency heat. If the house is cold, check the thermostat mode and the breakers for both the main unit and the auxiliary heat. If the system runs but blows cool air in heating mode, or if an alarm or fault light is showing, note it and call.

Staying warm and protecting pipes while you wait

  • Never use the oven, stove, an outdoor grill or a generator indoors for heat. They can produce carbon monoxide.
  • Space heaters: plug them straight into a wall outlet, keep them well away from anything that can burn, and don't leave them running unattended or while you sleep.
  • Close off unused rooms and keep doors and curtains shut to hold heat in the rooms you're using.
  • Protect your plumbing: if it's very cold out and the heat will be off for a while, open cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls and let a faucet drip slowly to help keep pipes from freezing.

For businesses and rental properties

In a shop, office or apartment building, no heat affects everyone, and unheated spaces can put water lines and fire sprinkler piping at risk of freezing. Let tenants or staff know, keep exterior doors closed, and call as soon as you know the heat is out. What to tell us when you call

  • System type: furnace, boiler, heat pump or geothermal, and whether it runs on gas, oil, propane or electric.
  • What it's doing: not turning on at all, running but blowing cold, short-cycling, making noise, or showing an error light.
  • What you've checked: thermostat, breaker, switch, filter, oil level or reset button.
  • Your address and a good callback number.
