How General Contractors Can Get Remodel Estimates Out Faster

Most remodel bids aren't lost on price. They're lost because the estimate showed up two weeks after the walkthrough, and by then the homeowner had already started talking seriously with someone else. Here is a simple system for getting a clear, written number in front of the customer while they're still excited about the project.

Why the slow estimate loses the job

A homeowner planning a kitchen or a bathroom usually calls more than one contractor. After the walkthroughs, they're waiting on paper. The first estimate that arrives becomes the yardstick every other bid gets measured against, and the contractor who sent it is the one the homeowner has been talking to most.

Meanwhile, most estimates get written at the kitchen table at night, after a full day on a job site. That's why they slip. The fix isn't working later. It's taking the repeat work out of each estimate so that writing one takes a fraction of the time.

Get the scope on the very first call

A lot of estimate delay starts before the walkthrough: you show up without knowing what they really want, and you end up needing a second visit or a string of texts to fill in the gaps. Whoever answers the phone should collect the basics every time:

  • Project type and rooms. Full gut, or cabinets and counters only? One bathroom or two?
  • Address and whether they own the home. It saves a wasted trip to a tenant who can't sign.
  • Timeline. Are they hoping to start next month or just getting numbers for spring?
  • What they've already picked. Have they chosen tile, fixtures or cabinets, or will they want allowances?
  • Photos. Ask them to text a few pictures of the space before you come out.
  • Budget range, if they offer one. Don't push, but write it down when they share it.

Walking into the house already knowing this means the walkthrough is for measuring and confirming, not discovering.

Build a price book you reuse on every job

If you price each remodel from scratch, every estimate is a fresh project. Instead, keep a price book: a list of the line items you sell over and over with your own price for each. Think demolition and haul-away by room or square foot, drywall, tile by the square foot, cabinet install by the linear foot, a standard bathroom fan swap, a toilet set, paint by the room.

Start with your last ten finished jobs. Pull the line items you used, write down what you actually charged and what it actually cost you, and set a number you're happy with. Update it whenever material prices move. After that, most of an estimate is picking items and entering quantities.

Use allowances for the parts nobody has decided yet

Homeowners often haven't picked their tile, vanity or lighting when they ask for a price. Don't hold the estimate while they shop. Put in a clear allowance for each undecided item, say what it covers, and explain that the final price moves up or down with what they choose.

Do the same with unknowns behind the walls. Name them on the estimate, for example rot under the subfloor or old wiring that needs replacing, and say how you'll price that work if you find it. It keeps the estimate fast and protects you from eating surprises.

Give yourself a turnaround rule and protect the time

Pick a promise you can keep, such as an estimate within two business days of the walkthrough, and tell the homeowner that before you leave the house. Then block the time on your calendar the same way you'd block time for a job. If you can, draft it in the truck right after the walkthrough while the measurements are fresh, and finish it the next morning.

Make the estimate easy to say yes to

A long, confusing estimate creates questions, and questions create delay. Keep it to a clear scope of work, the line items or sections with prices, the allowances, what's not included, the payment schedule, and a place to sign. If the homeowner can read it on their phone, approve it and send a deposit without printing anything, you remove the last excuse to put it off.

Measure your own speed

You don't need anyone else's numbers to know whether this is working. For the next month, write down three dates for every remodel lead: the day they first called, the day you did the walkthrough, and the day the estimate went out. Then note which ones you won.

Add up the days from first call to estimate sent and divide by the number of estimates. That's your average turnaround. Compare the jobs you won with the ones you lost. If the wins mostly went out fast and the losses mostly went out slow, you've found where your money is going.
