How cleaning businesses can collect a deposit before deep cleans and move-out cleans

You blocked out a whole day for a move-out clean, loaded the van, drove across town, and nobody was there to let you in. A simple deposit policy keeps that from happening, and most serious customers don't mind paying one when you ask the right way.

Figure out what a no-show actually costs you

Before you decide on a deposit amount, work out what a lockout or a same-day cancellation costs your business. Take the hours your crew was booked for, multiply by what you pay them per hour, and add your drive time, fuel and any supplies you bought or set aside for the job.

Then add the part most owners forget: the job you turned down because that slot was taken. If another customer asked for the same day and you said no, that lost booking belongs on the bill too. Write the total down. That number is your starting point for a deposit, because the deposit's job is to cover you when the appointment falls through.

Decide which jobs need a deposit

You don't need to ask every customer for money up front. Your weekly and biweekly regulars have already shown they're reliable, and asking them for a deposit can feel like a step backward. Focus on the jobs where a cancellation hurts the most:

  • Deep cleans. They take longer, often need two people, and usually fill a big piece of the day.
  • Move-out and move-in cleans. The timing depends on closings, landlords and moving trucks, so dates shift and keys go missing.
  • Post-construction cleans. Dust, debris and a schedule that depends on a contractor finishing on time.
  • First-time customers. You don't know them yet, and they don't know you.
  • Your busiest dates. The weeks before holidays and the end of the month, when every slot you hold is one you could have filled.

Pick an amount that's easy to say out loud

There are two common ways to set it. A flat amount is easy to explain on the phone and easy to remember. A percentage of the quote scales with the size of the job, which makes more sense if your jobs range from a small apartment to a large house.

Whichever you choose, check it against the no-show cost you worked out above. If your deposit covers only a small slice of what a lockout costs, it isn't doing much. If it's so high that it scares off good customers, bring it down. Make sure the deposit is credited toward the final bill, so the customer sees it as paying early, not paying extra.

Put the policy in writing before you hold the date

Most deposit arguments come from something that was never said clearly. Put these points in your quote or booking confirmation, in plain words:

  • What's included. Rooms, appliances, inside cabinets, windows, baseboards, whatever you do and don't cover.
  • The deposit amount and that it comes off the final bill.
  • Cancellation notice. How many days ahead someone has to cancel or reschedule to get the deposit back or move it to a new date.
  • Access and lockouts. How you'll get in (key, lockbox, door code, someone home) and what happens if your crew shows up and can't get inside.
  • Condition of the home. For move-outs, whether the place needs to be empty of furniture and belongings when you arrive.

Say the date is held only once the deposit is paid. That one sentence does a lot of work: it gives the customer a reason to pay now, and it gives you a clear rule for when to release the slot.

Make paying the deposit take one minute

The harder it is to pay, the more deposits you'll be chasing. Send a payment link by text the same day you quote, and accept the methods your customers already use. Give a deadline, such as the end of the next day, and tell them you'll open the slot to someone else after that.

If a customer misses the deadline, send one friendly reminder before you release the date. Plenty of people just forgot, and a short text is often all it takes.

Get the quote out while the customer is still deciding

A deposit only happens after a quote, and the quote only happens if you get back to the caller. Someone planning a move-out clean is usually calling more than one company the same afternoon. The business that sends a clear price first often gets the job.

Speed things up by asking the same questions on every call: the address, the number of bedrooms and bathrooms, the rough square footage, whether it's a regular, deep, move-out or post-construction clean, any pets, and the date they need. Ask for a few photos by text if you can't do a walk-through. With that list you can often price the job the same day.

What to say when someone pushes back

Keep it short and calm. Something like: "We hold the whole morning for your clean and turn other customers away for that time, so we ask for a deposit to hold the spot. It comes off your final bill." Most people understand once they hear that the deposit protects a real block of time.

If someone still refuses, you can offer them a later date on a waitlist without holding a slot. You don't have to argue, and you don't have to give away your best days for free.
