How Electricians Can Get EV Charger and Panel Upgrade Estimates Out Faster

EV chargers and panel upgrades are some of the best-paying jobs an electrician gets, and homeowners usually call two or three shops before they pick one. The electrician whose estimate shows up first, clear and complete, has a real head start. Here is how to get there without spending your evenings writing quotes.

Why these estimates stall

Most delays don't come from the pricing. They come from missing information. You take the call between jobs, jot down a name and a number, and later realize you don't know the panel size, where the car parks, or whether the house has ever had its service upgraded. Now you need a second call, or a site visit just to look, and the homeowner is already talking to someone else.

Fix the intake and the estimate usually gets easy.

Ask the same questions on every first call

Write a short intake script and use it every time, whether you answer, your spouse answers, or someone in the office does. For an EV charger or panel job, these cover most of what you need to price it:

  • Name, cell number, and the job address. Confirm the address is the house where the work happens, not a mailing address.
  • What they want. A Level 2 charger, a panel upgrade, both, or “I just bought an EV and don't know what I need.”
  • Panel size and location. Ask them to look at the main breaker: is it marked 100, 150 or 200? Is the panel in the basement, garage, or outside?
  • Where the charger goes. Garage, driveway, or outdoor wall, and roughly how far that spot is from the panel.
  • Charger already bought? If they have one, get the brand and model so you know what you're installing.
  • Age of the home. Roughly when it was built, and whether the electric has been updated.
  • Photos. Ask them to text a photo of the panel with the door open and a photo of where they want the charger.

Those two photos alone often answer the question of whether you can quote from the driveway or need to come out first.

Build a price menu instead of starting from zero

Most EV charger and panel jobs fall into a few common setups. Look back at your last ten or twenty of these jobs and group them. For example:

  • Charger in an attached garage, panel in the same garage, room in the panel.
  • Charger on a far wall or outdoors, longer wire run, room in the panel.
  • Charger where the panel is full and needs a load calculation or a subpanel.
  • Full panel replacement or service upgrade, with or without a charger.

For each setup, write down your typical labor hours, materials, permit cost in the towns you work, and your price. Add line items for the common extras: each extra foot of wire beyond a base run, trenching, drywall access, a surge protector. Now most estimates are a matter of picking the setup and adding the extras, not building a quote from a blank page.

Keep it honest. Your menu is a starting point, and the estimate should say what it assumes, such as panel location and wire run, so the customer knows the price can change if the site is different.

Send it the same day

Set a simple rule: any estimate you can price from the intake and photos goes out before you finish for the day. If you need a site visit, offer the customer two specific times on that first call back instead of “I'll get back to you.”

A good EV or panel estimate is short and plain: what you'll do, what's included (permit, inspection, materials, cleanup), what's not included, the price, and how to say yes. Homeowners comparing three quotes tend to trust the one they can understand in a minute.

Make it easy to say yes and put down a deposit

If the customer has to print, sign, scan and mail something back, the job can sit for a week. Send the estimate by text or email with a way to approve it on their phone, and ask for a deposit when they sign. A deposit gets the job on your schedule, covers materials like a new panel, and tells you the customer is serious.

Figure out what slow estimates cost you

You don't need an industry study to see if this matters for your shop. Go back through the last few months and count the EV charger and panel estimates you sent. Note how many days passed between the first call and when the estimate went out, and which ones turned into jobs.

Then look at the ones that went quiet. If the slow ones won less often than the fast ones, multiply the number of lost estimates by your average ticket for these jobs. That is roughly what the delay is costing you each quarter, and it gives you a real number to weigh against any tool or time you put into fixing it.
