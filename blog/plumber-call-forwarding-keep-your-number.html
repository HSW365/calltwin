How Plumbers Can Forward Calls Without Changing Their Business Number

Your phone number is painted on the truck, printed on every magnet and invoice, and listed on Google. You should never have to change it just to get your calls answered. Call forwarding lets you keep the number and still make sure somebody picks up when you are under a sink.

Why your number matters so much

For a plumbing business, the phone number is one of the most valuable things you own. Past customers keep it on the fridge. Neighbors pass it along. Your reviews, your directory listings and your Google Business Profile all point to it. Changing it means reprinting everything and hoping the old listings get updated, and some of them never will.

So when owners look at answering services, receptionists or AI answering, the first question is usually: do I have to give out a new number? In most cases the answer is no. You forward the calls from the number you already have.

How call forwarding works, in plain terms

Call forwarding is a setting on your phone line that sends incoming calls to a different number. The customer dials the same number they always have. Your carrier quietly passes the call along to wherever you told it to go: your cell, a helper's phone, an answering service or an AI receptionist.

On many landlines and some cell carriers, you turn forwarding on by dialing *72 followed by the number you want calls sent to, and turn it off with *73. Other carriers use different codes or have you switch it on in an app or online account, and business phone systems usually have a forwarding setting in their dashboard. The quickest way to be sure is to ask your carrier or search their support page for “call forwarding.”

Pick the right kind of forwarding

There are usually two ways to set it up, and the difference matters for a plumber:

  • Forward all calls. Every call goes straight to the other number and your phone does not ring. This works well for nights, weekends, vacations, or days you are on a long job and know you cannot answer.
  • Forward when busy or unanswered. Your phone rings first. If you do not pick up after a set number of rings, or you are already on a call, it forwards. Not every carrier or plan offers this, so ask. It is a good fit if you like answering yourself when you can and only want backup for the calls you miss.

A lot of owners use both: conditional forwarding during the workday, then all calls forwarded after hours so nobody has to wait through rings at 2am with water on the floor.

Set it up and test it before you rely on it

Forwarding is simple, but a five-minute test saves you from finding out on a busy day that calls were going nowhere.

  • Turn it on using your carrier's code, app or account settings.
  • Call your business number from a different phone, ideally one that is not in your contacts, and make sure the call reaches the right place.
  • Check your voicemail. If you use forward-when-unanswered, your carrier's voicemail can sometimes grab the call before forwarding kicks in. If that happens, ask your carrier about changing the ring time or turning off voicemail on that line.
  • Ask about charges. Most plans include forwarding, but some charge for it or for the minutes. One question to your carrier settles it.
  • Turn it off and test again. Make sure you know how to get your calls back to your own phone whenever you want.

Decide where the calls should go

Forwarding only moves the problem if the calls land somewhere nobody answers. Before you switch it on, decide who is actually picking up:

  • Your cell or a helper's phone. Free, but it is the same problem as before: when you are working, you are not answering.
  • A family member or office person. Great when they are available, but not at night, and not when they are busy with something else.
  • An answering service. A live person takes a message. Ask how they handle plumbing emergencies and what details they write down.
  • An AI receptionist. Answers right away, any hour, and asks the same questions every time.

Whichever you choose, give them a short script for emergencies. A caller with a burst pipe should be told where the main shutoff usually is and to turn it off while they wait. A caller who smells gas should be told to leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

Figure out if it is worth it for your shop

You do not need anyone else's numbers to decide. Open your phone's call log and count the calls to your business number over the last two weeks that you did not answer, leaving out spam. Take out the ones you called back and booked. What is left are the callers who most likely hired someone else.

Multiply that by your average plumbing ticket from your invoices, then by 26 for a rough year. Compare that number to what an answering option costs you. That is your answer, based on your own business.
