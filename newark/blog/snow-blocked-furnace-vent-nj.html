Snow blocking your furnace or boiler vent? What to do.

Many newer furnaces, boilers and water heaters don't use a chimney. They breathe in and exhaust out through plastic pipes on the side of the house, often just a foot or two above the ground. When snow drifts or ice builds up over those pipes, the heat can shut off, or exhaust can have nowhere to go. Here's how to check them after a storm in Warren, Hunterdon, Sussex, Morris or Somerset County, and when to stop and call.

Safety first

If you smell gas, don't touch light switches, thermostats or the equipment. Leave the building right away and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

If a carbon monoxide alarm goes off, or people in the house have headaches, dizziness, nausea or feel unusually tired, get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911. Don't go back in to clear a vent or check the furnace. Let the fire department tell you when it's safe.

Shoveling and working around the house after a storm is also a slip hazard. If the vents are on a steep, icy or hard-to-reach side of the house, leave it and call.

Why snow matters for your heating system

A high-efficiency (condensing) furnace or boiler usually has two pipes through an outside wall or the roof: one pulls in air for burning, the other pushes exhaust out. Some water heaters vent the same way. These systems are built to sense a blocked pipe and shut themselves down rather than run unsafely.

That safety shutdown is a good thing, but it means a snowdrift can leave you with no heat or no hot water on the coldest night of the week. And if the exhaust side is only partly blocked, combustion gases can back up. That's why working carbon monoxide alarms matter all winter.

How to find your vent pipes before you need to

  • Go to the furnace or boiler and look for two plastic pipes (often white) running from the unit to an outside wall or up through the roof.
  • Follow them to where they come out of the house. They might end in two separate pipes, a single combined fitting, or short pipes turned down or sideways.
  • Look for a small drain line too. Some systems run a condensate line outside, and it can freeze.
  • Note where the gas meter is. It's worth keeping clear of snow for the same reasons.
  • If your system vents into a chimney instead, the outdoor pipes don't apply, but the chimney still needs to be open and in good shape.

After a storm: how to clear the vents safely

  • Clear by hand or with a soft brush. Pull snow away from the vent openings and the area around them. Don't hit the pipes with a shovel, ice scraper or snowblower. Plastic vent pipe can crack, and a cracked exhaust pipe is a real problem.
  • Leave room around them. Clear more than just the opening so blowing snow doesn't seal it again. Keep the snowblower from throwing snow toward that wall.
  • Watch for ice. Exhaust from a condensing system is warm and wet, so frost or ice can build up at the opening in very cold weather. Gently brush off loose frost. If thick ice has formed inside or around the pipe, don't chip at it. Call.
  • Check from the roof line, too. Snow sliding off a roof can bury vents below it. Never climb onto a snowy roof to reach a roof vent. Call instead.
  • Don't cover the vents. Don't put buckets, boxes, tarps or homemade shields over vent pipes to keep snow off. Anything that changes how the pipe breathes can cause the system to shut down or vent poorly.

If the heat went off and the vent was blocked

Once the vent is clear, many systems will try to restart on their own the next time the thermostat calls for heat. Some need to be reset. You can turn the thermostat down and back up, and check the system's power switch is on. If you're not sure how your system resets, don't guess, and don't keep resetting it over and over.

Call a technician if the system still won't stay running, if it shut down and you never found a blockage, if the vent pipe looks cracked, sagging or disconnected, or if you see water, soot or scorching around the unit.

While you wait, keep faucets on outside walls dripping slightly and open cabinet doors under sinks, so pipes are less likely to freeze. Never use an oven, grill or outdoor heater indoors for warmth, and keep any space heater away from anything that can burn.

Don't forget the gas meter and other outdoor equipment

  • Keep snow and ice off the gas meter and the regulator on the gas line. Clear it gently by hand or with a brush, and don't kick or chip ice off it. If ice is built up on it, call your gas utility.
  • Make sure the dryer vent isn't buried, and that nothing is blocking the area around a heat pump or geothermal system's outdoor parts.
  • If you have oil heat, keep the fill pipe and vent pipe for the tank reachable and clear so deliveries can be made.
  • Check that snow piled against the house isn't blocking basement windows or vents that bring air to equipment rooms.

For businesses and rental properties

Shops, offices and multi-unit buildings often have several furnaces, boilers or rooftop units venting out the side or top of the building. Add the vent locations to your snow-removal plan, and tell whoever plows and shovels which pipes to keep clear and not to pile snow against. Landlords should make sure every unit has working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms before winter sets in.
