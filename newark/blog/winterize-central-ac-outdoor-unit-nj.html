Getting Your Central AC Ready for Winter in Warren County: Cover It, Clean It or Leave It Alone?

Once the leaves are down and the heat is on, most people stop thinking about the air conditioner until the first hot day in May. A few minutes of attention now, before snow and ice arrive, makes that first spring start-up a lot less likely to end in a service call. Here is what's worth doing, what to skip, and the one question to answer first.

First: is it an air conditioner or a heat pump?

The box outside looks the same either way, but the winter advice is opposite. A central air conditioner only cools, so it sits idle all winter. A heat pump cools in summer and heats in winter, so it has to keep running when it's cold out.

If you're not sure which you have, look at the thermostat for settings like "emergency heat" or "aux heat," check the label on the outdoor unit, or watch whether the outdoor fan runs when the heat is on. When in doubt, treat it as a heat pump until someone confirms. Never cover a heat pump or shut off its power for the winter.

Geothermal systems are different again. Their equipment is indoors and the loop is underground, so there is no outdoor unit to winterize. If you have geothermal, see our geothermal page for service.

Step 1: Clear around the outdoor unit

Leaves, clippings and twigs packed against the fins hold moisture against the cabinet all winter.

  1. Turn the system off at the thermostat before you do anything near the unit.
  2. Pull leaves and debris away from the sides by hand or with a soft brush. Don't bend the thin metal fins.
  3. Look down through the fan grille on top. If leaves have fallen inside, leave the panels on and have a technician clean them out. Don't reach in.
  4. Cut back shrubs and plants so there's open space around every side of the unit.
  5. Make sure the unit still sits level on its pad and that downspouts don't empty right onto it.

A garden hose on gentle spray can rinse dirt off the outside of the coil fins. Skip pressure washers. They flatten the fins and can force water into electrical parts.

Step 2: Cover it the right way, or not at all

This is where a lot of homeowners overdo it. Air conditioner outdoor units are built to sit outside through rain and snow. Wrapping one tightly in a plastic tarp can trap moisture inside, which encourages rust and corrosion, and a sealed, sheltered box is an inviting winter home for mice and other animals that chew wiring.

If you want to keep leaves, ice and falling icicles off the top, a cover that only goes over the top of the unit and leaves the sides open is the safer choice. A piece of plywood weighted down on top does the same job. Just remember to take it off before you turn the AC on in spring.

Again: none of this applies to a heat pump. A heat pump needs open airflow all winter.

Step 3: Decide about the outdoor power switch

Most outdoor units have a disconnect switch in a small box mounted on the wall nearby. For an AC-only system, some homeowners shut it off for the winter so the unit can't be switched on by accident on a mild day.

If you do this, be aware that many outdoor units have a small heater that protects the compressor and needs power. In spring, turn the disconnect back on and wait about a day before you run the AC, unless your equipment's manual says otherwise. If you're not sure, leave the power on and simply keep the thermostat on heat.

Don't open the disconnect box beyond its switch or handle, and never work on the unit's wiring yourself.

Step 4: Check inside, too

  • Filter: in most homes the furnace and the AC share one blower and one filter. Heating season is a good time to put in a fresh one and check it regularly.
  • Condensate drain: the AC drips water from the indoor coil into a drain line or a small pump. If that line runs through a cold garage or outside, make sure it's empty and not clogged, so it doesn't freeze or overflow next summer.
  • Condensate pump: if you have one, look for water around it or a pump that runs and runs. That points to a blockage.

If you have a heat pump: winter watch list

  • Keep snow, ice and drifting leaves clear of the outdoor unit. It needs air moving through it to heat your home.
  • It's normal to see the unit steam or go quiet for a few minutes in cold, damp weather. That's a defrost cycle.
  • It's not normal for the unit to stay encased in ice, for the fan not to run, or for the house to keep falling behind the thermostat. Call for service.
  • Clear snow away carefully by hand. Don't chip ice off the coil with tools.

If the AC struggled this summer

If the house never felt fully cool, ice formed on the copper lines, or water showed up around the indoor unit, get it looked at now rather than on the first heat wave.

Safety first

Turn the system off before you touch the outdoor unit, keep your hands out of the fan, and leave electrical parts to a licensed technician. If you smell gas anywhere in the house while you're checking the furnace area or filter, leave right away, get everyone out, and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Don't flip switches on the way out. Call a contractor only after the utility says it's safe.
