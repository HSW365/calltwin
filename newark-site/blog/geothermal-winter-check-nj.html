Is Your Geothermal System Ready for Winter? A Fall Check for Western NJ Homes and Businesses

Geothermal systems are known for being steady and low-maintenance, which makes them easy to forget about. But a heat pump still has filters, controls, a ground loop and a backup heat source, and the first cold night is a bad time to find out one of them needs attention. Here's what owners in Warren, Hunterdon, Sussex, Morris and Somerset counties can check themselves this fall, and what to leave to a technician.

Why a fall check matters for geothermal

A geothermal heat pump does the same job all year: it moves heat between the ground and your building. In summer it pushes heat out into the ground loop. In winter it pulls heat from the loop and brings it inside. The switch from cooling to heating is when small problems tend to show up, because the system is suddenly running longer and harder than it did in the mild weeks of fall.

Most of what goes wrong is ordinary: a clogged filter, a thermostat setting that changed, a drain line that backed up over the summer, or backup heat that kicks on more than it should. Catching those now keeps the system efficient, which is the whole reason you have geothermal in the first place.

What you can safely check yourself

None of these steps require opening the heat pump cabinet or touching the ground loop piping. If something looks wrong, stop and call.

  • Change or clean the air filter. A dirty filter is the most common reason a heat pump runs long and delivers lukewarm air. Check it now and keep checking it through the winter. Your owner's manual or the label on the filter slot tells you the size and how often to change it.
  • Switch the thermostat to heat and test it. Set it a few degrees above room temperature and listen for the system to start. Within a few minutes you should feel warm air at the registers. If your thermostat uses batteries, replace them now instead of on the coldest night of the year.
  • Look at the auxiliary or emergency heat indicator. Many geothermal thermostats show when backup heat is running. Seeing it once in a while on a very cold morning can be normal. Seeing it come on all the time, or seeing the system stuck in emergency heat, is worth a call.
  • Check for water around the unit. The heat pump makes condensation in cooling season. If the condensate drain clogged over the summer, you may see water on the floor or a damp base. Call if you see it.
  • Look at the unit's display or status lights. Many heat pumps have a light or code that shows a fault or lockout. If you see one, write it down before you reset anything. That information helps a technician find the problem faster.
  • Listen for new noises. Grinding, banging, rattling or loud humming that wasn't there before should be looked at before winter, not after.

What to leave to a technician

Some parts of a geothermal system aren't homeowner jobs. Leave these to a licensed professional:

  • The ground loop and its fluid. Closed loops are filled and pressurized when the system is installed. Low pressure or a leak in the loop isn't something to top off or patch yourself.
  • Refrigerant and electrical components. Anything inside the cabinet involves refrigerant and line-voltage electricity.
  • Repeated lockouts or resets. If the system keeps shutting itself down, resetting it over and over can hide a real problem. Turn it off and call.
  • Backup heat that never shuts off. If auxiliary heat is carrying the house, the heat pump may not be doing its job. The house may still feel warm, but you'll notice it on your electric bill.

Warning signs that shouldn't wait

  • The house won't reach the thermostat setting even though the system runs constantly.
  • Air from the vents feels cool while the system says it's heating.
  • Emergency heat is on and you didn't turn it on.
  • Water is collecting around the heat pump or in the mechanical room.
  • A breaker for the system keeps tripping. Don't keep resetting it.
  • A burning or electrical smell from the unit or vents. Turn the system off at the thermostat and call.

If your building also has a gas or oil appliance, like a water heater, a boiler or a furnace used as backup heat, the usual safety rules still apply. If you smell gas, don't flip switches or try to find the leak. Get everyone out and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get into fresh air and call 911.

If the heat goes out on a cold night

Check the simple things first: the thermostat is set to heat, the batteries aren't dead, and the breakers for the heat pump and the auxiliary heat haven't tripped. If your thermostat has an emergency heat setting and the heat pump has stopped, emergency heat can keep the building warm until a technician arrives. Use it as a stopgap, not a fix, and tell the technician you switched it on.

If the house is getting cold and you can't get heat back, protect your pipes. Open cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, and if you have a safe space heater, keep it clear of anything that can burn and never leave it running unattended. Then call.

Commercial buildings with geothermal

Offices, shops and other commercial buildings on geothermal follow the same checklist, with more at stake when a cold weekend hits an empty building. Before the season, make sure someone knows where the thermostats and system controls are, who to call after hours, and how the building is heated if the heat pump goes down.
