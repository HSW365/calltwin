How Landscapers Can Handle Snow Removal Calls When a Storm Hits

If you plow or shovel in the winter, the first real storm is the busiest your phone will be all year, and it rings at the exact moment you are in the truck. A little planning now decides whether those calls turn into paying customers or into a full voicemail box.

Decide who you will take before the first flake

Every storm you are really running two businesses: the seasonal or per-push customers you already promised to clear, and the brand-new callers who wake up to a buried driveway and start dialing. Your contract customers come first. That is what they paid for, and they are the ones who will renew next year.

So before the season starts, figure out how many extra stops your route can honestly absorb after your regulars are done. Maybe it is a handful of driveways near your existing route, maybe it is none during a big storm. Write that number down. It keeps you from saying yes to everyone on the phone and then letting down the people who already trust you.

Know what to ask every caller

A snow call is only useful if it has enough detail to price and route it. Make a short list and use it every time, whether you, a family member or a service is answering:

  • Name and callback number, read back to them so you are not chasing a wrong digit later.
  • The exact address, including a cross street. Mailbox numbers disappear under snowbanks.
  • Residential or commercial, and roughly how big: a one-car driveway, a long shared drive, a small lot, a plaza.
  • What they want cleared: driveway only, walkways and steps, the sidewalk out front, salt or ice melt.
  • Any deadline, like a business that opens at 7am or someone who has to get to work or a medical appointment.
  • Obstacles: cars that will be in the drive, a gate, a steep slope, a dog in the yard.
  • One-time or all season. This is the question that turns a one-off call into a contract.

Notice what is not on the list: a price. Quoting over the phone during a storm, before you know the size of the drive or how deep it got, is how you end up clearing a long shared driveway for the price of a short one.

Don't run your storm from the driver's seat

Picking up while you are pushing snow is unsafe, and even hands-free, every conversation slows the route down. At the same time, a caller with a buried driveway rarely leaves a message. They go back to the search results and call the next name on the list until somebody answers.

You need a plan for the phone that doesn't depend on you. Some options:

  • A family member or office helper takes calls from home with your question list in front of them. Works well if someone is around at 4am.
  • A traditional answering service takes messages around the clock. Make sure they have your list and know not to promise times or prices.
  • An AI receptionist answers every call at once, asks the same questions every time, and texts you the details so you can sort them at your next stop.

Whatever you pick, set it up and test it before the first storm, not during it.

Sort the calls when you stop, not while you drive

Pull over between stops, or at the end of a run, and go through the messages in a batch. Put each new caller into one of three piles: fits on the route this storm, can't take this storm, or wants seasonal service. Call the first pile back with a time window and a price. Call the second pile back too and tell them plainly you are full for this storm. That honest call costs you a minute and is often what gets you the customer next time.

The third pile is the most valuable one. Someone asking about the whole season is asking for recurring revenue.

Turn storm callers into seasonal contracts

The best time to sell a snow contract is the day after someone had to shovel their own driveway at 5am. When you call back the people you couldn't fit in, offer them a spot on next storm's route as a seasonal or per-push customer. Have a simple written agreement ready: what gets cleared, what depth triggers a visit, how you bill, and what happens with salt.

Collect payment or a deposit up front for seasonal service. It confirms the customer is serious, covers your fuel and salt early in the season, and means you aren't chasing invoices in March.

Figure out what a storm is worth to you

No industry average will tell you this; your own phone will. After the next storm, open your call log and count the calls that came in from people you didn't already serve. Skip spam and repeat calls from the same number. Then multiply:

New storm callers × the share you could actually have fit on the route × your typical per-push price.

That is roughly what one storm's phone calls are worth in one-off work. Then count how many of those callers might have signed up for the season, and multiply that by your seasonal price. That second number is the one that tells you whether answering the phone during storms is worth paying for.
