How Roofers Can Catch Roof Leak Calls During Storms and After Hours

When the wind picks up or the rain won't quit, your phone lights up with homeowners watching water drip through a ceiling. Those calls come at night, on weekends and while your crews are busy. The homeowner is scared and in a hurry, and whoever calls back first usually gets the tarp job, the insurance inspection and the new roof. Here is how to set up your company so storm and after-hours leak calls turn into work instead of voicemails.

Sort leak calls before the storm hits

Treating every storm call as an emergency burns out your crew. Most fall into a few groups:

  • Active leak inside. Water coming through a ceiling, light fixture or around a chimney right now. This homeowner wants someone today, or at least a tarp.
  • Visible damage, no leak yet. Missing shingles in the yard, a lifted ridge cap, flashing peeled back, a tree limb on the roof. Urgent, but it can usually wait for daylight.
  • Insurance and inspection calls. The homeowner has filed a claim or plans to, and wants a roofer to look before the adjuster comes.
  • Shoppers and follow-ups. People getting a second price, or past customers checking on a warranty question.

The first three are new jobs. Write down which group gets what response so anyone answering the phone, including you at 11pm, gives the same answer every time.

Set a tarp and emergency policy you can keep

Decide ahead of time what your company actually does after dark. Some roofers send a crew out for emergency tarping any hour. Many don't go up on a wet roof at night at all, for good safety reasons, and schedule tarps for first light. Either is fine. What hurts you is having no answer.

Put your policy in one or two sentences, for example: we don't climb roofs at night or in high wind, but every leak caller gets a call back by a set time and a tarp crew at first light, and we tell them how to limit damage inside until then. Decide too whether you charge for emergency tarping, so nobody on the phone has to guess.

Give homeowners something to do tonight

A frightened caller calms down when they have a task. Keep a short script of safe, inside-only tips you are comfortable giving, such as:

  • Move furniture and electronics away from the drip and put down buckets and towels.
  • If water is near a light fixture or outlet, stay clear of it and shut off that circuit at the panel if they can do it safely.
  • Take photos and video of the damage inside and out (from the ground) for the insurance claim.
  • Do not climb on the roof, especially in wind, rain or the dark.

These tips cost nothing and make it more likely they wait for your call instead of dialing the next roofer.

Capture the details that make the callback quick

Whatever is answering your phone after hours, make sure these get written down:

  • Name, best callback number and the property address
  • Whether water is coming inside right now, and where (which room, near a chimney, skylight, vent or wall)
  • What they can see outside: missing shingles, debris, a tree or limb on the roof
  • Roof type if they know it: shingle, flat or low-slope, metal, tile, slate
  • Whether they have called their insurance company or plan to
  • Whether they are the owner or a tenant

With that list, your morning triage takes minutes. Active leaks go to the top, and crews show up with the right materials.

Fix your voicemail for storm season

If voicemail is your after-hours plan, update the greeting before the season starts. Say that you handle storm damage and leaks, tell callers what details to leave (name, number, address, where the water is coming in), give one or two safety tips, and say exactly when you return calls. Then keep that promise.

Be realistic, though: plenty of homeowners with water on the floor won't leave a message. They hang up and call the next roofer in the search results.

Run a storm-morning callback routine

After a big storm the first hour of the day decides a lot of jobs. Before crews roll, have one person go through every message, text and missed call from overnight and call back in order: active leaks first, visible damage second, inspection requests third, everything else after.

End every callback with a specific next step: a tarp crew window, an inspection time, or a clear note of when you'll call back if you're booked solid. Homeowners can live with waiting; what sends them elsewhere is silence.

Work out what storm calls are worth to you

You don't need industry numbers; your own records tell the story. After the next storm, or using your call log from the last one:

  • Count every call that came in after hours or while you couldn't pick up, including hang-ups with no message.
  • Estimate how many were real leak or damage calls rather than shoppers or status checks.
  • Look at your invoices for what a typical tarp or repair job brings in, and what share of those turn into a larger repair or replacement.
  • Multiply the likely jobs you missed by that average. That is roughly what one storm's unanswered calls cost you.

Even if you are conservative, that number tells you how much it is worth spending to make sure those calls get answered.
