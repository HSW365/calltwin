How HVAC Companies Can Follow Up on Furnace and AC Replacement Quotes

A system replacement is the biggest ticket most HVAC shops write, and it is also the quote homeowners sit on the longest. They want to talk it over, get another price, or wait to see if the old unit limps through one more season. A steady, polite follow-up routine is often the difference between a signed job and a quote that quietly goes to the company that called back.

Why replacement quotes stall

A repair call is urgent: no heat, no cooling, fix it today. A replacement is a decision. The homeowner is looking at a number with a comma in it, comparing equipment names they've never heard of, and probably getting two or three other quotes. Most of them aren't saying no. They're busy, unsure, or waiting for someone to make the next step easy.

That's good news for you. If most stalled quotes are undecided rather than lost, a clear follow-up plan wins jobs you already paid to estimate: the drive, the load calculation, the hour at the kitchen table.

Find out what your open quotes are worth

Before you build a routine, see what's sitting in your pipeline. Pull every replacement quote you wrote in the last three months and sort them into three piles: signed, turned down, and never heard back. Add up the dollar value of the never-heard-back pile.

Then work out your own close rate: signed quotes divided by total quotes written. If you nudged just one or two of those silent quotes into signed jobs each month, multiply that by your average replacement ticket and by twelve. That's the yearly value of a better follow-up habit, using nothing but your own numbers.

Make the quote easy to say yes to

Follow-up works better when the quote itself is clear. Before you worry about the calls, look at what you're leaving behind:

  • Good, better, best. Two or three clearly labeled options let people choose instead of deciding whether to say yes at all.
  • Plain language. Spell out what's included: removal of the old unit, permits, thermostat, line set, warranty, and what's not included.
  • What happens next. Say how to accept, how much deposit you need, and roughly how soon you can install.
  • One name and number. The homeowner should know exactly who to call with questions, and that person should actually answer.

A simple follow-up schedule

You don't need software to start. A notebook, a spreadsheet or your calendar works. What matters is that every quote gets the same treatment and nothing depends on remembering.

  • Same day: Send the written quote by text or email before you're back in the truck, with a short note thanking them and inviting questions.
  • Two or three days later: Call, don't just text. Ask if they had a chance to look it over and whether anything was unclear. Questions about financing, brands or install dates usually come out here.
  • About a week later: Send something useful instead of a nudge: a short explanation of the options, rebate or financing information you actually offer, or a reminder of your install schedule.
  • Two to three weeks: Ask directly and kindly: “Are you still deciding, did you go another way, or should I close this out?” People appreciate the honesty, and a no frees you to stop chasing.
  • Seasonal check-in: For quotes that stalled because the old system is still running, put a note on your calendar for the start of the next heating or cooling season.

What to say on the follow-up call

The goal is to help, not to pressure. Lead with their situation: “How's the old furnace holding up?” Then ask what's holding the decision. If it's price, walk through the options again and make sure they're comparing the same scope of work as the other quotes. If it's timing, tell them honestly how your schedule looks. If it's the spouse who wasn't home for the visit, offer to answer their questions by phone.

Avoid fake deadlines and scare tactics. Homeowners compare notes, and the company that was straight with them is the one they call back and recommend.

Don't lose the callback

Here's the part most shops overlook: the follow-up works, the homeowner calls you back to say yes or ask a question, and nobody picks up. In the heating season you're on a roof or in a crawlspace half the day. If that call goes to voicemail, the homeowner may take it as a sign of how hard you'll be to reach after the install, and call the next quote on the pile.

So treat incoming calls as part of your follow-up system. Make sure your office line is covered when you're on jobs and after hours, and that every message lands somewhere you'll see it quickly.
