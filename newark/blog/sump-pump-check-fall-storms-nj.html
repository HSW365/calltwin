Sump Pump Check Before Fall Storms in Warren and Hunterdon County, NJ

Heavy autumn rain, wet leaves and the first snowmelt of the season all send water toward your foundation. If your basement depends on a sump pump, the time to find out it doesn't work is on a dry afternoon, not at 2 a.m. in a downpour. Here is a simple check any homeowner or building manager can do, and what to do if the pump has already failed.

Safety first

A sump pit sits in the lowest part of the basement and the pump plugs into an outlet nearby, so water and electricity are close together. Before you touch anything:

  • Never reach into the pit or stand in water while the pump is plugged in. Unplug it first, or switch off its circuit at the panel if you can reach the panel without standing in water.
  • If the basement is already flooded and water is near outlets, cords, the electrical panel or appliances, stay out and get help.
  • If you smell gas anywhere in the house, especially near a furnace, boiler or water heater that has been sitting in water, leave right away and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

1. Look at the pit and the pump

Take the cover off the pit and use a flashlight. You want to see a pump sitting upright on a firm base, a float switch that can move freely, and no mud, gravel or debris piled around the intake. Check that the discharge pipe coming out of the pump is connected tightly and that the check valve on it, the fitting that keeps pumped water from running back into the pit, is in place.

Look at the power cord too. It should be plugged directly into an outlet, not run through a long extension cord, and the cord should not be cracked or frayed.

2. Run a test with a bucket of water

With the pump plugged in and your hands dry, slowly pour a bucket or two of water into the pit until the float rises. The pump should switch on by itself, lower the water quickly, and shut off once the float drops. Listen while it runs.

  • Pump doesn't start: check that it's plugged in and that the outlet or breaker hasn't tripped. If it still won't run, the switch or the motor may have failed.
  • Pump hums but no water moves: the intake may be clogged or the impeller jammed.
  • Pump runs but won't shut off: the float may be stuck. Unplug it and call a plumber.
  • Loud grinding, rattling or burning smell: unplug it. Those are signs of a pump that is wearing out.

3. Follow the water outside

Go outside while the pump runs and find where the discharge line ends. Water should flow freely and land well away from the foundation, sloping away from the house. In fall, the end of that line is easy to bury under leaves. Clear it, and clear nearby gutters and downspouts too, because water from a clogged gutter can pour right back down beside the foundation and into the pit.

Before winter, make sure the outside end of the line can drain. A discharge line that holds water can freeze in a cold snap, and a pump pushing against a frozen line can't get water out of the basement.

4. Think about backup

The storms that make a sump pump work hardest are often the same storms that knock out power. If a pump is the only thing keeping your basement dry, ask a plumber whether a battery backup pump, a second pump or a high-water alarm makes sense for your home or building. An alarm is a simple way to find out the water is rising before it reaches the floor.

If the pump has already failed and water is coming in

  1. Stay out of standing water if it's near anything electrical. If you can safely reach the panel from a dry spot, turn off power to the basement.
  2. If you smell gas, leave and call your gas utility or 911.
  3. Move valuables, boxes and anything stored on the floor to higher ground if you can do it safely.
  4. Don't run the washing machine, dishwasher or showers. That water often drains to the same place.
  5. Take photos of the water and any damage for your records.
  6. Call a plumber. A failed sump pump during heavy rain is an emergency.

Homes and commercial buildings

The same check applies to offices, shops, rental properties and other commercial buildings with a sump pit or basement pump. If a building has more than one pump or a pump in a mechanical room, add it to your fall maintenance list alongside the heating system and any fire sprinkler lines, and make sure whoever is on site knows where the pumps are and who to call.
