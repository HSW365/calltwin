Cold Radiators or Banging Pipes? Boiler Heat Problems in Warren and Hunterdon County Homes

Plenty of older homes in Phillipsburg, Washington, Clinton and Flemington heat with a boiler and radiators or baseboard. When the cold weather settles in, two complaints come up again and again: some rooms stay cold while others are fine, and the pipes bang, knock or gurgle. Here's what those symptoms usually mean, what you can safely check yourself, and when to stop and call.

Safety first

If you smell gas, don't touch light switches, the thermostat or anything that could make a spark. Get everyone out of the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone into fresh air and call 911 before doing anything with the heating system.

Radiators, pipes and the boiler itself get hot enough to burn. Let things cool before you touch valves or vents, and keep children away while you work. If water or steam is coming out of the boiler's relief valve, shut the boiler off and call. Never plug, cap or tie down a relief valve.

First, know which kind of system you have

The fixes are different, so it matters which one is in your basement.

  • Hot water (hydronic): the boiler has a pressure and temperature gauge, and the house has radiators or baseboard with water circulating through them, usually moved by a pump called a circulator. Radiators often have a small square-key bleed valve near the top.
  • Steam: the boiler has a glass sight tube showing the water level, and the radiators have a small metal air vent on the side, often shaped like a bullet or cylinder, that hisses as the radiator heats up.

Some radiators cold on a hot water system

The most common cause is air trapped in the system. Air collects at high points, so rooms on upper floors or at the far end of a loop are often the first to go cold.

  • Check the thermostat and zones first: many homes have more than one zone. If a whole floor is cold, make sure that floor's thermostat is calling for heat before blaming the radiators.
  • Feel the radiator: a radiator that is warm at the bottom and cool at the top usually has air in it. One that is cold everywhere may have a closed valve or a zone that isn't opening.
  • Clear furniture and covers: baseboard and radiators need air flowing over them. Long curtains, furniture pushed against them and heavy dust between the fins all cut heat output.

If you're comfortable doing it, you can bleed a radiator. With the system off and cooled, hold a rag and a small cup under the bleed valve, open it slowly with a radiator key or screwdriver, and close it as soon as water comes out steadily instead of air. Then check the boiler's pressure gauge. Bleeding lets water out, and many systems refill automatically, but some don't. If the pressure reads low or keeps dropping, don't try to add water yourself unless you know how your system is set up. Write down the reading and call.

Banging or knocking pipes

On a steam system, banging is often called water hammer. It happens when water collects in pipes or radiators that should be carrying steam, and the steam slams into it.

  • Radiator valves: the valve at the bottom of a steam radiator should be either fully open or fully closed. A half-open valve can trap water and cause banging.
  • Radiator pitch: steam radiators need to tilt slightly so water can drain back toward the pipe. If a radiator was bumped or the floor has settled, it can hold water.
  • Air vents: a vent that never hisses, or one that spits water, may be stuck or failing. A cold steam radiator with a working valve often points to a bad vent.
  • Sight glass: note where the water sits in the glass. Too high or too low can both cause problems, but adding or draining water is best left to a technician if you aren't sure what the right level is for your boiler.

On a hot water system, knocking and ticking are often pipes expanding as they heat, while gurgling usually means air. Loud, sudden bangs, rumbling from the boiler itself, or noise along with leaks are reasons to call rather than wait.

Signs it's time to call instead of troubleshooting

  • Water on the floor: a puddle around the boiler, under a radiator or along a baseboard needs attention. Shut the boiler off if you can do it safely.
  • Pressure problems: the gauge on a hot water boiler is reading well above or below where it normally sits, or keeps changing.
  • Relief valve discharging: water or steam coming from the relief valve or its pipe.
  • Whole floors or zones cold: this can point to a circulator, zone valve or control problem rather than air.
  • The boiler is short-cycling, shutting down or showing a fault light: write down any code or blink pattern so the technician comes prepared.

For landlords and businesses

In apartment buildings, offices and shops, one cold room often means a tenant or employee calling you. Ask exactly which rooms are cold and whether pipes are making noise, and don't let anyone shut radiator valves halfway or cover vents. Cold spaces against outside walls can also put water lines and fire sprinkler piping at risk of freezing. What to tell us when you call

  • System type: hot water or steam, and whether it runs on gas, oil or propane.
  • What you're seeing: which rooms or floors are cold, what the noise sounds like and when it happens.
  • Gauge readings: the pressure on a hot water boiler or the water level in a steam boiler's sight glass.
  • What you've checked: thermostats, valves, bleeding, and anything you've changed.
