Carbon Monoxide and Your Furnace or Boiler: Heating Season Safety in Warren County, NJ

The first cold nights in Warren, Hunterdon and Sussex County mean furnaces and boilers that sat idle all summer are running hard again. Any heating system that burns gas, oil or propane makes carbon monoxide as part of combustion. When the system and its venting work the way they should, that gas goes up the flue and outside. When something is wrong, it can end up in your living space, and you won't see it or smell it. Here's what homeowners and building managers should know before winter settles in.

If your CO alarm goes off or someone feels sick

Treat it as an emergency. Do not try to find the source first.

  1. Get everyone, including pets, out of the building into fresh air right away.
  2. Call 911 from outside. Tell them a carbon monoxide alarm is sounding or that people have symptoms.
  3. Do not go back inside until emergency responders say it is safe.
  4. If anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or unusual sleepiness, tell the responders and get medical attention.
  5. Once the building is cleared, have the heating system and any other fuel-burning appliances checked before you use them again.

If you smell gas at any point, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside. Don't flip light switches, use the phone inside, or start a car in an attached garage on your way out.

Why heating season raises the risk

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, so the only reliable way to know it is present indoors is a working CO alarm. Heating systems are a common source because they run for long stretches in a closed-up house. Problems that can let it escape include:

  • A blocked or disconnected flue or vent pipe, including a chimney partly blocked by debris or a nest that built up over the summer.
  • A cracked heat exchanger in a furnace, or a damaged section in a boiler.
  • Burners that are dirty or out of adjustment and are not burning cleanly.
  • Exterior intake or exhaust vents on high-efficiency systems that get covered by leaves, mulch or, later in the season, snow and ice.
  • Not enough fresh air for combustion, for example a furnace closed into a small room after a basement remodel.

Warning signs at the furnace or boiler

You can't detect CO itself, but you can notice signs that a heating appliance is not venting or burning properly. Call a heating technician if you see any of these:

  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner area, the furnace cabinet, or the base of the vent pipe.
  • Flame color changes on a gas burner. A healthy flame is mostly blue. A lazy, mostly yellow or orange flame can point to a combustion problem.
  • Rust, water streaks or corrosion on the vent pipe or where it connects to the chimney.
  • A vent pipe that has come loose or sags so the joints don't line up.
  • Stale, stuffy air or heavy condensation on windows near the system or in rooms that are closed up.
  • Symptoms that ease when you leave the house such as headaches or feeling flu-like at home but better at work or outdoors. Treat this as an emergency and follow the steps above.

What you can safely check yourself

  • Make sure you have CO alarms on every level of the home and near sleeping areas. Press the test button and replace batteries as the manufacturer directs. Alarms also have an end-of-life date printed on them; replace any that are past it.
  • Walk outside and look at the furnace or boiler vent and intake pipes. Clear away leaves, mulch, stored items and, after a storm, snow, without bending or loosening the pipes.
  • Keep the area around the furnace or boiler clear. Don't store paint, gasoline, cleaning chemicals or piles of boxes next to it.
  • Change the furnace filter if it is dirty. A clogged filter makes the system work harder.
  • Never use a gas oven, grill, camp stove or generator to heat the house or garage, even for a short time with a door open.

Leave the inside of the furnace or boiler, the burners, the gas valve and the flue connections to a qualified technician. Taking panels off or adjusting parts on a fuel-burning appliance can make a hidden problem worse.

Why a fall heating check matters

A heating check before steady cold weather is the best time to catch combustion and venting problems, while it is still easy to schedule and before the system is running all day. A technician can inspect the burners, look at the heat exchanger or boiler sections, check that the flue is drafting properly, and confirm the system is getting enough combustion air. Businesses, rentals and older buildings

Landlords, property managers and business owners in Warren, Hunterdon, Sussex, Morris and Somerset County carry extra responsibility, because tenants and employees may not know where the heating equipment is or what a warning sign looks like. Make sure every unit and work area has working CO alarms, keep mechanical rooms clear, and have commercial heating equipment looked at before the heating season. Older buildings that have been renovated over the years sometimes end up with vent runs or combustion-air openings that no longer match the equipment, which is worth having a technician review.
